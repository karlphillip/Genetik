# P0 — Mathematical representations for the nth prime

## Objective and standard

The target is the exact indexed function `p_n`, with `p_1 = 2` and `p_1000 = 7919`. A useful result must produce the *nth* prime, run independently, and be compact and computationally feasible. A prime-valued sequence, a formula that recognizes primes, and an algorithm that enumerates primes are different results. Exact agreement on a finite prefix does not establish an identity for all positive indices.

The review is analytical. No synthesis, evolutionary, or large benchmark campaign was run. Prior prime-regression evidence is relevant only as evidence about Genetik's existing search: the stored S15 exact-expression run found four matches in its 1,000 training rows, zero matches in 50 additional assessment rows, exhausted its 56,758-structure storage allowance, and produced no exact rule. The earlier floating-point campaign was approximate regression and examined indices 1001–1200; that range is not untouched confirmation. See `docs/PRIME_REGRESSION.md` and the S15 evidence package.

## Families reviewed

| Family | What is established | Exactness and cost | Relevance to constructive discovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prime-counting inverse | By definition, `p_n = min{x : pi(x) = n}`. Exact algorithms compute `pi(x)`; the prime number theorem only gives asymptotic estimates such as `p_n ~ n log n`. | Computing `pi` by a sieve and inverting it is practical for this target, but explicitly enumerates/counts candidate primes. Analytic `pi` methods require substantial specialized algorithms and precision. | Excellent correctness/runtime baseline, not a qualifying discovered formula under the no-hidden-enumeration rule. Existing scalar arithmetic expressions do not provide a prime-counting primitive; adding one would conceal the core operation.
| Ruiz–Sondow floor/divisor-count formula | Ruiz and Sondow give exact floor/arithmetic formulas for `pi(x)` and hence `p_n`, proved using Rosser–Schoenfeld bounds. Their optimized divisor count `d(j)` checks divisors through `sqrt(j)`; `d(j)=2` detects primes. | They report `O(x^(3/2))` for `pi(x)` and `O((n log n)^(3/2))` for `p_n`. At `n=1000` their prime-index bound is about 13,818 and the divisor-check total is around one million. | Computationally feasible, but its actual mechanism explicitly tests divisors of every candidate integer and sums the resulting prime indicator. Treat it as an exact enumeration algorithm in formula notation, not a new discovery under the stated restriction.
| Wilson/Willans factorial indicator and nth-prime formula | Wilson's theorem gives a divisibility characterization of primality. Willans derives an exact nth-prime formula using a prime indicator and nested finite sums. This is a proven formula, not an approximation. | The published form sums an outer index through `2^n` and uses inner sums up to that index. The number of inner summands is `2^n(2^n+1)/2`; at `n=1000`, this is about `5.74e601`. Its factorial-based indicator also requires huge exact integers or an equivalent modular computation. | Mathematically explicit but computationally unusable in the stated form. Its key operation is an encoded primality test repeated over candidate integers. Genetik's checked Int64 factorial overflows above `20!`, and its bounded aggregation is not a practical replacement for the required modular-factorial/primality computation.
| Mills-type prime-representing constants | Mills proved existence of a real constant `A > 1` such that `floor(A^(3^k))` is prime for every positive `k`, using prime-gap results. This generates a sparse prime-valued sequence, not `p_k`. | Assuming the commonly quoted least Mills constant `A ≈ 1.3063778838630806904686144926` (its stated identification/value is conditional on the Riemann hypothesis), `A^(3^1000)` has approximately `1.53e476` decimal digits (about `0.386*3^1000` bits). The constant is constructed using a selected chain of primes, so its precision carries information about that chain. | Fails the required output semantics and practical output size. Treating a fitted high-precision constant as a compact discovered rule would hide target information and precision cost.
| Diophantine prime representations | Jones, Sato, Wada and Wiens exhibit a degree-25 polynomial in 26 variables whose positive values are exactly the primes. This is a theorem about the set of values with existentially varying integer arguments. | It does not order those values by `n`, give a bounded witness for the nth prime, or supply an efficient witness search. Enumerating argument tuples and sorting/deduplicating outputs adds an unbounded search problem. | Impressive representability result, poor constructive route to `p_n`. A finite bounded search cannot certify absence of witnesses, and the polynomial's large expression is not an operational nth-prime program.
| Prime-gap recurrences | The identity `p_{n+1}=p_n+g_n` is exact when `g_n` is the actual prime gap. No compact generally valid low-order integer recurrence for the gap sequence is established by the reviewed material. | Storing observed gaps is a lookup table; computing the next gap by testing candidate integers is explicit prime enumeration. | Genetik S16–S18 recurrence experiments recovered several generic controls but provide no mechanism or evidence for discovering exact prime gaps. A fit to a finite prefix is especially vulnerable to alternative recurrences.
| Bounded sums/products and floor identities | Finite sums can express counts, and products can encode divisibility or congruence conditions. These are mathematically meaningful constructs. | Exact primality indicators derived from factorials, inclusion-exclusion or products shift the cost into large integer arithmetic, many terms, or loops over candidate divisors. At Int64 bounds, factorial and product growth quickly invalidates direct evaluation. | The syntax may be compact while its evaluation is not. A bounded sum operator alone does not supply an efficient prime rule; it can merely wrap a sieve or primality test.
| Generating functions / zeta explicit formulas | Analytic identities relate prime counts to zeta zeros, Möbius inversion and prime-power sums. Their mathematical content is established under their stated convergence/limiting conditions. | Exact finite evaluation needs truncation, error bounds and high-precision arithmetic; inversion to an individual prime still requires locating where the count crosses `n`. | Not compatible with Genetik's exact Int64 expression semantics, and an approximate truncated value does not certify the integer output without rigorous interval/error control.

Continued fractions were not promoted to a candidate family: the reviewed sources supplied no concrete continued-fraction identity with exact indexed `p_n` semantics and a bounded cost argument. A fitted real constant or its continued-fraction digits would require an independent information/precision audit and risks merely encoding the target.

### Mathematical status

- **Proven:** `p_n` is the inverse of the nondecreasing prime-counting function in the minimum-crossing sense; Willans' formula is exact; Mills' theorem gives a prime-representing function; the Jones–Sato–Wada–Wiens polynomial represents the set of primes; the prime number theorem is asymptotic.
- **Not established here:** a compact low-cost formula discoverable from the first 1,000 labels in Genetik's existing grammar; a low-order prime-gap recurrence; or a useful finite-data identification guarantee.
- **Conjecture/assumption:** none is used to claim exact output. Some Mills constant identifications in the literature rely on hypotheses or computed prime chains; the unconditional existence theorem does not make the resulting sequence equal the indexed prime sequence.
- **Oracle/algorithm boundary:** a prime indicator, `pi(x)`, or a hidden sieve can produce exact answers, but if its implementation searches/classifies candidate integers it is a prime-generation algorithm, not a newly discovered closed mathematical rule.

## Discoverability in Genetik

The current exact evaluator has checked Int64 arithmetic and rich generic constructs (including modulo, GCD/LCM, bounded sum/product/first, factorial, powers and conditionals). That expressiveness does not make large formulas feasible: node/storage/depth/work limits apply, arithmetic overflows explicitly, and finite training values underdetermine many expressions. S15's nth-prime expression search is direct evidence: its best training candidate was `(x0 + (1 + (-2 % x0)))`, matched only 4/1000 training points and 0/50 additional assessment points, and stopped at storage exhaustion. The output was not an exact rule.

S8–S18 repeatedly showed that structural diversity, parameter fitting, enumerative banks and conditional recurrence decomposition do not by themselves identify useful mathematical structure. The valid S18.1 result was narrow and limited to selected recurrence families; S18.3/S18.3R confirmation claims were invalidated by protocol/configuration defects and remain diagnostic only. None supplies evidence that an expression tree containing generic integer operators can construct an efficient sieve, prime-counting algorithm, or compact prime-gap law within bounded search.

In particular, the expression language's bounded aggregate operators are *evaluators*, not a general low-cost stateful loop compiler. A formula whose source is short can perform astronomical iteration or require unrepresentable intermediates. Representation size, arithmetic bit complexity, range of intermediate values, and output size must all be charged.

## Comparative assessment

The best three candidate routes are:

1. **Ruiz–Sondow exact floor/divisor-count formula.** It is an indexed exact formula and computationally feasible at `n=1000`. Its obstacle is that its divisor-count subroutine directly tests divisors to classify candidate integers, then counts the primes; that is an explicit primality-enumeration algorithm in formula notation, not an acceptable discovery under this mission. Minimal falsification of suitability: inspect its published `d(j)` subroutine, which explicitly loops over divisors through `sqrt(j)`; this already establishes the disqualifying mechanism, so no implementation run is needed.
2. **Willans' exact formula.** It meets indexed semantics and demonstrates formal expressibility. Its obstacle is a lower-bound-sized iteration count of roughly `5.74e601` inner summands at `n=1000`, before factorial arithmetic. Minimal falsification: compare the exact term-count expression at small `n` and extrapolate analytically; the `n=1000` bound already rejects practical use without implementation.
3. **A low-order prime-gap recurrence.** If such a compact independently executable law were found, it could be fast and exact. The obstacle is that no such law is known from these data, while observed gaps can be memorized and finite prefixes do not identify a recurrence. Minimal falsification: freeze a small grammar and train only on a prefix, then require exact autonomous continuation on new indices and alternate starting contexts. Given S15 and the previous recurrence studies, this has low prior plausibility and should not be run without new mathematical structure.

The exact `pi` inverse/sieve baseline is also strong computationally but has the same enumeration boundary. Mills and Diophantine representations rank below these for this objective: one is sparse and output-size explosive; the other characterizes a set existentially but provides no practical index-to-witness map.
